## 1. Introduction

- **Hematology**: the study of blood (A2, B2).
  - Also addresses how blood parts / its parts are involved in health and disease (A2, B2).
- **Components of blood**, 4 items (A2 · A2n · B2–3):
  1. Red blood cells (RBCs)
  2. White blood cells (WBCs)
  3. Platelets
  4. Other proteins (notes: "involved in the bleeding and clotting cascades")
- **Hematopoietic system**: organs and tissues involved in the production of blood components (A3, B4).
  - Bone marrow, spleen, lymph nodes (A3); notes: "primarily bone marrow, spleen, and lymph nodes" (A3n · B4).
- **Hematologic disorder**: any disorder of the blood (A3, B4).
  - **Hemolytic disorders**: disease processes that cause the breakdown of RBCs (A3, B4).
  - **Hemostatic disorders**: bleeding and clotting abnormalities (A3, B4).

## 2. Blood and plasma

### 2.1 Blood basics (A4 · B5)
- Blood is a **connective tissue**.
- Blood is composed of **cells and cell fragments suspended in plasma**.
- Blood accounts for approximately **8%** of the total body weight: approximately **5 to 6 L**.

### 2.2 Primary functions of blood, 8 items (A5 · A5n · B6)
Slide wording first, then notes detail:
1. Supply oxygen and nutrients to cells.
2. Transport carbon dioxide and waste. Notes: "nitrogenous wastes from tissues to lungs and kidneys".
3. Carry hormones. Notes: "from the endocrine glands to the target tissues".
4. Regulate body temperature.
5. Regulate pH. Notes: "through the buffering components in the blood".
6. Balance fluid and electrolytes. Notes: "through sodium and plasma proteins".
7. Regulate the immune system. Notes: "through the actions of WBCs and antibodies".
8. Form clots. Notes: "through the action of platelets".

### 2.3 Two components of blood (A6 · A6n · B7)
- **Plasma**:
  - **92% water and 8% various solutes**. Notes list the solutes: proteins, electrolytes, clotting factors, glucose.
  - **55%** of total blood volume.
- **Formed elements**:
  - **45%** of total blood volume.
  - Include RBCs (**99%**), WBCs, and platelets.
  - Notes give the alternative names: RBCs (**erythrocytes**), WBCs (**leukocytes**), platelets (**thrombocytes**); "99% RBCs".

### 2.4 Figure: blood composition (A7 left · B8)
- **Labels**: Blood 8% of total body weight; 8% (band on abdomen); Plasma 55%; Water 92%; Solutes 8%; Leukocytes and thrombocytes (platelets); Formed elements 45%; Erythrocytes.
- Caption (notes): "components of a blood sample and the development of the cells of the hematologic system."
- ⚑ The alt text says "9 percent" for blood weight and "9 percent solute", but the figure itself and slide A4 say **8%**. The alt text is a typo, so the site uses 8%.

### 2.5 Figure: development of blood cells / hematopoiesis (A7 right · B8)
**Labels**, top to bottom:
- **Hemocytoblast** branches into proerythroblast, myeloblast, lymphoblast, monoblast, and megakaryoblast.
- **Proerythroblast → Polychromatic erythroblast → Erythrocytes**
- **Myeloblast → Progranulocyte → Basophil, Eosinophil, Neutrophil**. These three are grouped as **Granulocytes**.
- **Lymphoblast → Lymphocyte**
- **Monoblast → Monocyte**
- Lymphocyte + Monocyte are grouped as **Agranulocytes**.
- Granulocytes + Agranulocytes are grouped as **Leukocytes**.
- **Megakaryoblast → Megakaryocyte → Thrombocytes**

There are 5 pathways in all. This one figure is the source for both a sequence builder and a tap-to-label activity.

### 2.6 RBC production (A8 · A8n · B9)
- Production of RBCs occurs within **stem cells**. Notes: "cells that develop into other types of cells".
- Stimulated by **erythropoietin**, secreted by the **kidneys**. Notes: "a protein secreted by the kidneys in response to circulatory need".
- **Five days** to mature. Notes: "may take as long as 5 days".
- Average life span is **4 months** (slide); average life of **120 days** (notes). These are the same value.
- Oxygen-carrying **hemoglobin**. Notes: "Within RBCs, iron-rich hemoglobin carries oxygen to the tissues."
  - Oxygen attached to hemoglobin gives blood its red color (A8n · B9).
- **Bohr effect** (A8n · B9): when oxygen-rich RBCs encounter an environment with higher concentrations of carbon dioxide (more acidotic), oxygen is released.

### 2.7 Three common lab tests (A9 · A9n · B10)
1. **RBC count**: measures the number of RBCs in a blood sample.
2. **Hemoglobin level**: identifies the amount of hemoglobin found within the RBCs.
3. **Hematocrit measurement**: gives the overall proportion of RBCs in the blood.

Blood is considered **balanced** if:
- Hemoglobin level is **one-third** the hematocrit level.
- RBC count is **one-third** the hemoglobin level.

### 2.9 White blood cells (A11 · A11n · B12)
- **Larger** than RBCs.
- Provide **immunity against foreign invaders**.
- Derived from **stem cells**.
- Several types. Notes: "perform a specific task to maintain the immune system".
- Certain disease processes are also specific to the differential WBCs.
  - **Neutropenia**: an abnormally low number of **neutrophils**, which make up the **majority** of the circulating WBCs.
- Immune system responses are **humoral** or **cell-mediated**:
  - **Humoral immunity**: secretion of antibodies called **immunoglobulins**, which recognize a specific antigen (notes).
  - **Cell-mediated immunity**: **macrophages and T cells** attack and destroy pathogens or foreign substances (notes).

### 2.11 Platelets and hemostasis (A13 · A13n · B14–15)
- **Platelets**:
  - **Smallest** formed element.
  - Derived from stem cells.
  - Approximately **two-thirds** circulate through the blood; the rest are stored in the **spleen**.
  - **Clot the blood**. Notes: "responsible for clotting the blood":
    - Form the **initial plug** following a vascular injury.
    - Clotting proteins then toughen and complete the blood clot.
    - **Thrombocytosis**: the production of too many platelets, which can create **thrombosis**.
- **Hemostasis**: the process that stops bleeding (slide) / "allows the body to stop bleeding" (notes). Notes list 3 parts:
  1. Vascular spasm
  2. Coagulation
  3. Platelet plugging
  - ⚑ Standard references give the order as vascular spasm → platelet plug → coagulation. The notes list coagulation second. The site keeps the notes' list for the exam and shows the order note in a "Beyond your notes" box.
- **Clots are made of fibrin**:
  - Following injury, **thrombin converts fibrinogen to fibrin**, and the clotting process begins (notes).
  - **Calcium** acts as a binding agent (notes).
- **Clotting cascade**: the process by which clotting factors work together to (ultimately) form fibrin.

### 2.12 Figure: clotting cascade (A14 · A14n · B16)
- Notes: it is also called the **coagulation cascade**, and it can be initiated through either an **intrinsic** or an **extrinsic** pathway.
- **Coagulopathy**: any process that interferes with the activation or continuation of the clotting cascade or hemostasis. It can lead to heavy or prolonged bleeding, such as **von Willebrand disease** (A14n · B16).
- **Figure labels**:
  - Column headers: **Contact**, **Intrinsic**, **Extrinsic**, **Common pathway**.
  - **Contact**: Surface contact; XII → XIIa; XIIa → XI; XIIa → Prekallikrein → Kallikrein → Kinins → Inflammation.
  - **Intrinsic**: XI → XIa; IX → IXa with Ca⁺²; then VIII(AHF), Ca⁺², Platelet lipid.
  - **Extrinsic**: Tissue thromboplastin (III), released from damaged tissue; VII → VIIa with Ca⁺² and Tissue lipid.
  - **Common pathway**: X → Xa; Va, Ca⁺², Lipid; II → IIa; V → Va (activated by IIa); XIII → XIIIa (activated by IIa); I (fibrinogen) → Ia (fibrin) (activated by IIa); Ia (fibrin) → Insoluble fibrin (activated by XIIIa).

## 3. Blood-forming organs and RBC production (A15 · A15n · B17–18)

Major players in the hematologic system:

- **Bone marrow**:
  - Primary site for cell production.
  - Found in most of the long bones and the pelvis, skull, and vertebrae (notes).
- **Liver**:
  - Produces (the) clotting factors (found in the blood).
  - Filters blood.
  - Removes toxins.
  - Essential to normal metabolism and homeostasis (notes).
  - Breaks down old RBCs into **bile** (notes).
  - Highly vascular (notes).
  - Stores blood (notes).
- **Spleen**:
  - Also vascular (notes).
  - Filters and breaks down RBCs.
  - Assists with **lymphocyte** production (notes).
  - Helps provide homeostasis and infection control.
  - Stores **one-third of the platelets** (notes).

**Figure (A16 · B19)**: anterior torso. Labels: **Liver**, **Spleen**, **Bone marrow** (in the upper arm bone). Caption: "the major players in the hematologic system: the bone marrow, the liver, and the spleen."

## 4. Patient assessment

### 4.1 Overview (A17 · A17n · B20)
- Note any (immediately) life-threatening signs or symptoms.
  - Unusual bleeding or (apparently) uncontrolled hemorrhage can indicate an **underlying pathology**.
- Ask (in-depth, relevant questions) about the patient's history and **SAMPLE** history: Signs and symptoms, Allergies, Medications, Pertinent past history, Last oral intake, Events leading to injury or illness.

### 4.2 Scene size-up (A18 · A18n · B21)
1. Ensure the scene is safe for entry.
2. Consider the mechanism of injury.
3. Determine the number of patients.
4. Assess for hazards (notes: "and the need for additional help").
5. **Gloves, mask, and eye protection** are the standard minimum precautions (notes).

### 4.3 Primary survey (A19–A21 · notes · B22–26)
- Perform **cervical spine stabilization**, if necessary.
  - Don't dismiss a pain complaint of the spine just because it could be a manifestation of the disease state.
- Form a general impression:
  - Assess the **ABCDEs** (Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, Exposure).
  - Immediately correct any life-threatening issues.
  - Perform a rapid (full-body) scan and determine LOC.
- Airway and breathing:
  - Patients with inadequate breathing or altered mental status should receive appropriate **oxygen** therapy.
  - Be careful to **avoid causing bleeding** if additional airway support (suctioning and/or airways) is needed.
- Circulation:
  - Look for signs of shock, such as a **rapid pulse rate and low BP** (notes).
  - If **hemophilia** is suspected, watch for:
    1. (Signs of) acute blood loss
    2. (Any) bleeding of unknown origin
    3. (Signs of) hypoxia
  - **Avoid washing out clots**. Notes: "be careful not to wash out clots if fluid resuscitation is necessary"; "use caution with fluid administration".
  - Manage life-threatening conditions.
- Provide transport (to an appropriate facility).

### 4.4 History taking (A22–A25 · notes · B27–30)
- Obtain patient and SAMPLE history.
- Hematologic disorders may involve multiple symptoms that seem unrelated (at first glance). Examples:
  - **Pneumonia** in a patient with **sickle cell disease**.
  - **Abdominal pain** in a patient with **polycythemia**.
- Look for, 11 items (A23 · B28):
  1. Changes in LOC
  2. Vertigo
  3. Feelings of fatigue
  4. Syncope (notes: syncopal episodes)
  5. Dyspnea
  6. Chest pain
  7. Changes in pulse rate and rhythm
  8. Coughing up blood
  9. Visual disturbances
  10. Muscle pain
  11. Stiffness
- Are complaints part of a larger disease process, or unrelated (A24 · B29)?
- Is pain isolated, or felt throughout (the entire body)?
- Ask if the patient has experienced (A25 · B30):
  1. Skin changes
  2. Bleeding
  3. Pain for unknown reasons
  4. Genitourinary problems
  5. Gastrointestinal problems

### 4.5 Secondary assessment (A26 · A26n · B31)
- Performed on scene, en route, or not at all.
- Perform a physical exam.
- Recognize (some of the common) findings associated with blood disorders.
- Obtain (baseline) vital signs.

**TABLE 25-3: Common Findings With Blood Disorders** (A26 · B31), verbatim:

### 4.6 Reassessment (A27 · A27n · B32)
- Reassess frequently (to determine if the patient's condition has changed).
- Inform the hospital of:
  1. Patient history
  2. Present situation
  3. Assessment findings
  4. Interventions and results
- Thoroughly document the patient encounter.

## 7. Sickle cell crisis (A29–A34 · notes · B34–43)

### Pathophysiology
- The **most common inherited blood disorder**.
  - Primarily affects **African American, Puerto Rican, and European** populations (notes).
  - As of **2016**, approximately **100,000** people in the United States had sickle cell disease (notes).
  - Average life expectancy is **42 years for males** and **48 years for females** (notes).
- Begins with a gene defect of the adult-type hemoglobin (**HbA**).
  - Can be inherited from **both parents (HbSS)** or **one parent (HbS)**.
  - When inherited from both parents, there is a high probability offspring will have the disease or carry the mutation (notes).
  - ⚑ Standard references call inheritance from one parent "sickle cell trait (HbAS)". The site keeps the file's "HbS".
- Defective RBCs have an **oblong shape** instead of a smooth, round shape.
  - The shape makes the RBC a **poor oxygen carrier**, increasing risk of **hypoxia**.
  - Cells have a much **shorter life span**, increasing risk of **anemia**.
  - The odd shape can cause RBCs to lodge in small blood vessels, leading to **thrombosis** (notes).
- **Photos** (A29 · B34): a micrograph of normal disc-shaped RBCs, and a micrograph of normal plus sickle-shaped RBCs. Caption (notes): "normal red blood cells and sickle cells."

### Sickle cell disease can lead to (A30 · B36)
- **Aplastic crisis**: RBC production temporarily stops. Notes: "causing the patient to become easily tired, anemic, pale, and short of breath".
- **Hemolytic crisis**: acute RBC destruction leading to **jaundice**.

### Sickle cell crisis may manifest in several ways (A30–A31 · B36–39)
1. **Vasoocclusive crisis**. Notes: "blood flow to an organ becomes restricted".
   - Causes pain, ischemia, and often organ damage.
   - Usually lasts **5–7 days**.
   - Circulation to the **spleen** is frequently obstructed, due to its narrow vessels and its function of removing damaged RBCs.
2. **Acute chest syndrome**: vasoocclusive crisis associated with **pneumonia**.
   - ⚑ A31 says "associated with pneumonia **and PE**"; A31n, B36, and B38 say pneumonia only. Both are kept and flagged.
   - Common signs and symptoms: **chest pain, fever, and cough**.
   - Vasoocclusion in the brain may result in **stroke** (on slide B38 and in the notes; not on slide A31).
3. **Splenic sequestration crisis**: sickle cells within the spleen block blood from leaving the spleen.
   - Results in painful, acute enlargement of the spleen and a hard, bloated, painful abdomen.
   - Usually occurs in **infants or toddlers**.
   - **Acute splenic sequestration syndrome**: a life-threatening condition in which RBCs become trapped in the spleen.
     - Causes a dramatic fall in hemoglobin available in circulation.
     - Painful abdomen and sudden weakness, pallor, tachypnea, and tachycardia.
     - Can rapidly progress to **shock** [A only, A31].

### Assessment (A32–A33 · B40–41)
- Often a life-threatening crisis.
- Characterizations may include, 6 items (A32 and notes; B40's slide shows a shortened 4-item version):
  1. Shortness of breath
  2. Signs of pneumonia
  3. Inadequate perfusion of the skin, accompanied by hypotension
  4. Signs of jaundice
  5. Yellowing in the eye (**icteric sclera**)
  6. Mild dehydration
- Patients may have significant pain. Notes: "in acute crises, significant pain and multiple system involvement".
- **Pediatric** patients typically present with (initial) pain in the **hands and feet**.
- **Adults** typically report **back and proximal extremity** pain.

### Management (A34 · A34n · B42–43)
1. Place patient in a position of comfort.
2. Cover patient (with a blanket) to maintain body temperature. **Cold can contribute to sickling of cells.**
3. Administer supplemental oxygen.
4. Give IV fluid therapy (notes: "if necessary").
5. Administer analgesics per local protocol. Patients may have a **high pain threshold** and require a higher level of analgesia (notes).
6. Recommend that the patient rest (during transport).

## 8. Anemia (A35–A39 · notes · B44–51)

### Pathophysiology (A35 · B44)
- Low hemoglobin or RBC level (lower than normal).
- Usually associated with:
  1. Underlying disease (process)
  2. Blood loss, acute or chronic
  3. Decreased production or increased destruction of erythrocytes
  4. Hemolytic disorder related to the breakdown of RBCs (notes: "preexisting hemolytic disorder")

### Iron-deficiency anemia (A36 · B45)
- The **most common type** of anemia.
- Causes include:
  1. Gastrointestinal blood loss
  2. Menstrual bleeding
  3. (Blood loss from) frequent blood donations or diagnostic tests
  4. Low birth weight. Notes: "premature birth or low birth weight (in children)".

### Potential causes (A37 · A37n · B46–47)
- **Inherited hemolytic disorder** or a hematologic disorder. Notes: "sickle cell disease or thalassemia".
  - RBCs develop rigid and deformed membranes, which may become lodged in small blood vessels, leading to thrombosis (notes).
- Deficiency of **glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD)**:
  - Enzyme protection of RBCs during infections (notes).
  - Most common in **African Americans** (notes).
- Disruption of RBC flow from problems with blood vessel linings or blood clots (notes).
- Autoimmune disorders.
- Microorganisms.
- Can cause problems at **high altitudes**. Notes: "effects of smaller number of RBCs and reduced partial pressure of oxygen":
  1. Hypoxia
  2. Difficulty breathing
  3. Chest pain

### Assessment (A38 · A38n · B48–49)
- Patients may (most will complain of):
  1. Feeling worn down
  2. Lack of energy / having no energy
  3. Feeling overexerted / as if they overexerted themselves
  4. Being unable to catch their breath
- Patients may (also) have:
  1. **Anginal-type chest pain**, related to reduced oxygen supply to the heart (notes)
  2. Skin color changes
  3. **Leukopenia** (reduction in WBCs)
  4. **Thrombocytopenia** (low platelet counts), with:
     - Cutaneous bleeding (including **petechiae**)
     - Bleeding from mucous membranes (such as nosebleeds and rectal bleeding)

### Management (A39 · A39n · B50–51)
1. Allow the patient to assume a position of comfort.
2. Check and monitor the airway and breathing (closely).
3. Check vital signs frequently.
4. For chest pain, apply a **cardiac monitor**. A **12-lead ECG** may also be needed (notes).
5. Manage blood pressure and replace fluids if needed.
6. Consider **rapid transport** for:
   - Abrupt change in mental status
   - Hypotension
   - Other significant changes

## 9. Leukemia (A40–A44 · notes · B52–58)

### Pathophysiology (A40 · B52)
- Cancer in (that develops in) the **lymphoid system**.
  - ⚑ Standard references describe leukemia as cancer of the blood-forming tissues and bone marrow (myeloid *or* lymphoid). The site keeps the file's wording.
- Increased production of immature/abnormal blood cells (notes: "particularly WBCs"), causing:
  1. Anemia
  2. Thrombocytopenia
  3. Leukocytosis
- Chemotherapy (used) typically leads to **leukopenia** (decreased WBCs).
- Patients with leukemia experience: frequent bleeding, bruising, infections, and fever.
- **Photo** (A40 · B52): a child with leukemia reclining with a teddy bear (decorative, but can be used as a "who is this patient?" prompt).

### Acute vs chronic (A41 · A41n · B53)
- **Acute leukemia**: bone marrow is replaced with abnormal **lymphoblasts**.
- **Chronic leukemia**:
  - Develops more frequently in people **65 years or older**. This is on the slide in A41; in B it appears only in the notes.
  - Abnormal mature lymphoid cells accumulate in: (1) bone marrow, (2) lymph nodes, (3) spleen, (4) peripheral blood. B53's slide says "abnormal cells".
  - Typically found **by chance** during routine blood tests (notes).

### Survival and treatment (A42 · A42n · B54)
- Survival depends on:
  1. Stage of detection
  2. Underlying medical condition
  3. Response to treatment
- Treated with **chemotherapy and radiation** (therapy).
  - Typically (in most cases) results in **remission**.
  - Children with **acute lymphoblastic leukemia** have a **5-year survival rate of more than 90%** (notes).

### Assessment (A43 · A43n · B55–56)
- Use standard precautions, including **gloves and mask**: the patient could be **immunocompromised**.
- Patient presentation depends on: stage of leukemia; current treatment.
- Patients may present with (notes list 10; the slide shows 4, marked \*):
  1. Fatigue\*
  2. Headaches\*
  3. Dyspnea
  4. Signs of neurologic defects
  5. Fever
  6. Bone pain\*
  7. Diaphoresis
  8. Feeling full
  9. Soreness in the midpart of the chest
  10. Unexplained bleeding\*
- Assess all vital signs and the cardiac rhythm: **hypotension and tachycardia** are often present.

### Management (A44 · A44n · B57–58)
- As appropriate, provide:
  1. Airway support
  2. Oxygen therapy
  3. IV fluid therapy
  4. Analgesics
  5. Emotional support
- You may be called because (the patient's condition has deteriorated and) loved ones are uncertain about what to do:
  1. Discuss (the situation) with medical control.
  2. Document all findings (before leaving).
  3. Have a refusal/release form signed (if transport is declined).
- If you transport, be aware the patient could go into **arrest**. Make sure you understand the patient's and family's wishes about what to do [B only, B58 notes].

## 10. Lymphomas (A45–A50 · notes · B59–65)

### Pathophysiology (A45 · B59)
- A group of **malignant** diseases (that arise) within the **lymphoid system**.
- Classified in **two** categories: **Non-Hodgkin** and **Hodgkin**.

### Non-Hodgkin lymphoma (A46 · B60)
- Can occur at **any age**.
- Can be **hereditary**.
- Characterized by progression:
  1. **Indolent (very slow)**: may never leave the lymphoid system.
  2. **Aggressive**: "(Fast-Growing)" [A only].
  3. **Highly aggressive**: "(Very Fast-Growing)" [A only]; may affect multiple organs in a short period.

### Hodgkin lymphoma (A47–A48 · B61–62)
- **Painless, progressive enlargement** of lymphoid glands.
- Most commonly affects the **spleen and lymph nodes**.
- **Rare** form.
- Suspected to have some hereditary components (notes).
- Peak ages, "incidence at two peaks": **10 and 35 years**, and **late in life**.
- Symptoms may not occur for years, but include (slide 8 items / notes 9 items):
  1. Night sweats
  2. Chills
  3. Persistent cough
  4. Swelling of (various) lymph nodes
  5. Loss of appetite
  6. (Significant) weight loss. The slide combines 5 and 6 as "Loss of appetite or weight loss".
  7. (Generalized) itching
  8. Fatigue
  9. Bone pain
- With treatment, symptoms disappear for long periods, and cure is possible (notes).

### Assessment (A49 · A49n · B63–64)
- Lymphomas require (specialized levels of) **chemotherapy or radiation**.
- Response to treatment depends on the stage of the disease and its classification (notes).
- Ask about the type of cancer and treatment: "Which type of cancer do you have?" and "Which type of treatment are you receiving?"
- Signs and symptoms may include:
  1. Pallor
  2. (Some) congestion in lower lung fields
  3. Feeling hot and then cold, or both (in different areas of the body)
  4. Inadequate perfusion
  5. Low blood pressure
  6. Abnormal ECG rhythms

### Management (A50 · A50n · B65)
- Manage pain **aggressively** with a **high-dose analgesic regimen**.
- Treat inadequate perfusion (notes: "with fluid therapy, and provide supplemental oxygen").
- Treat abnormal heart rhythms.
- Initiate **rapid transport** if the condition does not improve (or deteriorates).
- You may be called to offer support but not transport (notes):
  1. Be supportive.
  2. Discuss your findings with medical control.
  3. Explain options to the patient and family, and allow them to make a decision.
- **Photo** (A50 · B65): two EMS practitioners treating a patient on a stretcher (decorative).

## 11. Polycythemia (A51–A54 · notes · B66–70)

### Pathophysiology (A51 · B66)
- **Overabundance or overproduction of RBCs**.
- Results in increased blood **viscosity** and **volume**.
  - Can result in congestion of tissues and organs (notes).
  - **Hyperviscosity** of the blood increases the risk of **thrombus formation**.
- Can be caused by:
  1. A **rare disorder**, originating from a single stem cell
  2. **Existing disease**: heart failure or hypotension
     - ⚑ Standard references give *chronic hypoxia* (e.g., lung or heart disease) as the cause of secondary polycythemia; "hypotension" is unusual. The site keeps the file's wording.
  3. Living in **high altitudes** for long periods

### Complications and treatment (A52 · A52n · B67)
- May lead to other conditions (slide 6 items / notes 7 items):
  1. Strokes
  2. Transient ischemic attacks
  3. **Deep vein thrombosis** (notes only)
  4. Pulmonary embolism
  5. Myocardial infarction
  6. Headaches
  7. Abdominal pain, usually associated with an **enlarged spleen** (this detail is on the slide)
- Treatment may include:
  - **Phlebotomy**, to maintain hematocrit levels (notes):
    - Less than **45% in men**
    - Less than **42% in women**
  - Cancer-type therapy

### Assessment (A53 · A53n · B68–69)
- Findings may vary widely:
  1. Altered levels of consciousness
  2. Hypoxia
  3. Respiratory distress
  4. Changes in peripheral pulses, pulse rate, and skin color
  5. Tachycardia
- Note the extent and duration of **dyspnea**.
- Has the patient experienced (uncontrolled itching) **pruritus**?
- Has the patient noted changes in **skin temperature**?
- Obtain a (thorough) medical history.

### Management (A54 · A54n · B70)
- Initially focus on the **chief complaint**. Although the underlying pathology may be polycythemia, the patient may be experiencing a different medical emergency (notes).
- (Otherwise,) supportive care and transport to the appropriate facility.
- Oxygen as needed.
- IV access as needed, for possible pharmacologic interventions.

## 12. Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) (A55–A57 · notes · B71–74)

### Pathophysiology (A55 · B71–72)
- A number of causes. Notes: "may be caused by a number of life-threatening conditions".
- Progresses in **two stages**:
  1. **First stage**: free thrombin and fibrin deposits in the blood increase.
     - Platelets begin to aggregate.
     - **Defibrination** (breakdown of the fibrin clots) occurs.
  2. **Second stage**: uncontrolled hemorrhage results from a reduction in clotting factors.
- Mortality rate is hard to determine:
  - Patients often have additional conditions (notes).
  - Death is related to uncontrolled bleeding, hypotension, and shock.

### Assessment (A56 · B73)
- Identify associated signs and symptoms (notes: "associated with DIC or progression toward it").
- Patients may have:
  1. (Episodes of) respiratory difficulty
  2. Signs of shock
  3. Skin changes: cold and clammy, to pallor, to small black-and-blue marks (**purpura**) on the chest and abdomen

### Management (A57 · B74)
1. Maintain an airway.
2. Administer (supplemental) oxygen.
3. Treat for shock.
4. Provide pain management and treat abnormal heart rhythms.
5. Be **optimistic but honest**.

## 13. Hemophilia (A58–A61 · notes · B75–78)

### Pathophysiology (A58 · A58n · B75)
- A bleeding disorder in which clotting does not occur or occurs insufficiently.
- Usually associated with an **X-linked recessive** inheritance pattern (notes).
- **Two** primary types:
  - **Type A**: low levels of **factor VIII** (antihemophilic globulin and antihemophilic factor).
  - **Type B**: deficiency of **factor IX** (plasma thromboplastin component / the **Christmas factor**).
- Primarily found in **males** (notes).
- Levels of factors VIII and IX determine the **severity** of the disease (notes).

### Signs and symptoms (A59 · B76)
- These are the same in both types (notes):
  1. Acute and chronic bleeding
  2. **Spontaneous intracranial bleeding**
- Patients (with significant acute bleeding episodes) may require:
  1. Hospitalization for transfusion
  2. Infusion of factors VIII and IX

### Assessment (A60 · B77)
1. Manage the ABCs.
2. Be alert for signs of acute blood loss.
3. Note any bleeding of unknown origin.
4. Watch for signs of hypoxia.
- Link back to the primary survey: avoid washing out clots with fluids (A21).

### Management (A61 · A61n · B78)
1. Administer oxygen if appropriate.
2. Note ECG findings; treat symptomatic dysrhythmias as appropriate (notes).
3. IV therapy may be necessary / if needed.
4. Analgesics may be appropriate.
5. Cover patients to maintain body temperature.
6. If the bleeding stopped before you arrived, suggest the patient seek **immediate follow-up** (notes).

## 14. Multiple myeloma (A62–A65 · notes · B79–82)

### Pathophysiology (A62–A63 · B79–80)
- The number of **plasma cells** in the bone marrow increases abnormally, forming **tumors in the bone**:
  - Impairs normal bone marrow function (on slide A62; in B only in the notes).
  - Decreased RBC, WBC, and platelet function. Notes: "formation".
  - Results in **anemia** and **susceptibility to infection**.
- Neoplastic cells may accelerate protein development in the bloodstream, which leads to **organ failure**.
- Occurs mostly in patients **older than 40** (notes).
- As the disease progresses, patients may have weakness in the bones:
  - Results in **spontaneous fractures**.
  - Pain in the bones and back.
- In advanced cases, **stem cell transplant, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy** may be given.

### Assessment (A64 · A64n · B81)
- Adhere to appropriate standard precautions, including **mask and gloves**, to help keep patients with multiple myeloma safe.
- Findings depend on the stage (of the disease):
  - Early-stage complaints: **fatigue and mild pain**.
  - Later stages:
    1. (Unexplained) hemorrhage
    2. (Significant) weight loss
    3. (Frequent) bone fractures
    4. Pain (in various locations)

### Management (A65 · B82)
1. IV fluid therapy
2. Pain management
3. Supportive care
4. Definitive care

## 15. Transfusion reactions (A66–A70 · notes · B83–90)

### Pathophysiology (A66 · A66n · B83–84)
- Transfusion reactions occur in approximately **0.2% to 10%** of blood transfusions (notes).
- Occur when a patient receives a blood type different than their own.
- Similar to **anaphylactic** reactions:
  - Occur rapidly.
  - Cause (severe) circulatory collapse (notes: "or death").
- Monitor a patient receiving a blood transfusion very closely for the **first 30 to 60 minutes**.
- Determine the patient's blood type and the type of blood received (notes).

### Assessment (A68 · A68n · B86)
- Symptoms can be **subtle** in patients who are **unresponsive or intubated**.
- An acute reaction may include a rapid onset of (slide 4 items / notes 6 items):
  1. Chills
  2. **Fever** (notes only)
  3. Back pain
  4. **Vomiting** (notes only)
  5. Tachycardia
  6. Hypotension
- Reactions may be **delayed up to 7 days** (notes).

### Complications: 6 categories (A69 · A69n · B87–88)
1. **Hemolytic reaction**:
   - The **greatest threat** to the patient during a blood transfusion.
   - Primarily caused by incompatibility between recipient and donor blood.
   - The recipient's immune system is activated by donor blood, destroying new RBCs.
2. **Febrile reaction**:
   - The **most common** complication.
   - Usually **benign**.
   - Treated with an **antipyretic and observation**.
3. **Allergic reaction**:
   - An anaphylactic reaction to **preservatives** or other agents in the product being transfused.
   - Usually occurs within the **first few minutes** of transfusion.
   - Classic signs and symptoms of an anaphylactic state.
4. **Transfusion-related lung injury**:
   - A **noncardiogenic pulmonary edema** caused by increased capillary permeability after transfusion.
   - Treatment focuses on supporting the **ABCs**.
5. **Circulatory overload**:
   - Typically occurs in patients with preexisting **cardiomyopathy or ventricular dysfunction**.
   - Treatment: relieve the system of its excess fluids through **diuresis**, and use **oxygen, nitrates, and morphine**.
6. **Bacterial infection**:
   - Typically a result of poor blood product handling or contamination during the infusion process.
   - Can lead to full systemic **sepsis**.

### Management (A70 · A70n · B89–90)
- Severity of the reaction correlates to the **amount of blood volume transfused** (notes).
- Care is centered on, 6 steps in order:
  1. **Immediately stop the transfusion.**
  2. Recheck donor blood: check identifying information to determine whether the wrong blood was given.
  3. Contact medical control.
  4. Provide supportive care (to counteract shock).
  5. Replace existing IV tubing and bag (with **normal saline**).
  6. Retain blood products and tubing, and transfer them to the hospital. This step is on the A70 slide and in the B90 notes; A70's notes stop at step 5.
